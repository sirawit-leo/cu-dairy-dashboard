Short-term Action Plan

แผนปฏิบัติการระยะสั้น — จำกัด 3 ถึง 5 ข้อ course pp.9, 92, 99

Five, and no more. p.9: “โดยเฉลี่ยแล้วเกษตรกรสามารถรับมือกับคำแนะนำในการเปลี่ยนแปลงการจัดการได้เพียงจำนวนหนึ่งเท่านั้น … เพียง 3 ถึง 5 ข้อ” — a farmer can absorb only a handful of management changes at once, so a good plan picks the three to five most urgent and moves everything harder into the mid- and long-term plan.

Each is stated as a SMART target (p.92) — specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time-related. The SMART statement is a restatement, not a new claim: every clause in it is one of the action's own fields, and the table underneath shows which. Four of the five are simply the top of this page's own priority ranking; the fifth is a deliberate departure, argued in Method.

The remaining ten actions. p.92 aims this plan at the “ต้นตอ (Root causes)” and at stopping the problem recurring, so they are grouped by the root cause they attack rather than by which domain they came from — the grouping that matters is which branch of the problem tree each one closes off.

Follow-up Timeline

กำหนดรอบติดตามผล — วงจร PDCA course pp.9, 93, 99

p.9: an action plan is not a document that ends when it is written — it enters a continuous improvement cycle (Plan · Do · Check · Adjust) and the vet must set an explicit date to come back, “เช่น กลับมาประเมินซ้ำในอีก 3-4 เดือนข้างหน้า”. p.93 puts the same cycle as Measure → Manage → Monitor.

The trap this timeline exists to avoid. A KPI has two kinds. A process KPI moves as soon as people comply — read it early, on purpose, because that is exactly when you want to know whether the thing is being done. An outcome KPI moves on biology's timetable, or when enough records accumulate to make a rate. An outcome read before its time reads “no change” for reasons that have nothing to do with whether the action works — and a plan that mistakes that for failure abandons a working action. Every round below separates the two.
Three waves of work, underneath the three rounds of measurement. First 30 days: costs nothing, needs nobody's permission. Days 30–90: a ration change, a rota, a service visit. Days 90–180: construction, and the hormone programme that should start only if the rest has not worked. An arrow in the bar means the action waits on another. Do not read a bar as a measurement window — the bar is when the work runs, the rounds above are when it is read.

Appendix — how these five were chosen

ภาคผนวก — วิธีจัดลำดับความสำคัญและหลักฐานอ้างอิง course p.102

Two of the six rows in the course's marking rubric are the quality of the Problem Tree and the feasibility and prioritisation of the action plan. This appendix is the working behind the second: the cost-against-impact matrix, the time-to-effect grid, all fifteen actions against all five criteria, the full ranking, and the provenance of every number on the page.

score = impact × urgency weight ÷ cost weight. Cost divides, urgency multiplies — so a medium-cost action needs ~2.6× the impact of a free one to rank alongside it. Impact is a 1–10 ranking score, not a forecast: how much of the herd's measured shortfall does this sit in front of? A pool addressed is not a saving projected.

Cost × impact — where to start

The darker the cell, the sooner it should be done. Cells holding no actions are dimmed — including the top-left corner, because nothing on this list is both free and impact 9–10. Click an action to open its card.

Impact × time-to-effect — what you will be able to see, and when

Time-to-effect is a planning estimate, not a measurement — nothing in this project measures it. It is how long before the outcome an action is aimed at could move at all, set by biology and by how fast the denominator fills at this herd size. It is not how long the work takes, and not when the action’s own compliance KPI moves. Cell figures are the reach: how many head the action sits in front of.
